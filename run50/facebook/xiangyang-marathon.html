Xiangyang gave me a marathon with a finish I did not see coming

A first race back in China after nearly six years turned into a fast-moving tour of a historic city - old walls, river bridges, Tang-style palaces, wuxia energy and a surprise 3:57 finish.

By Arsenan Race: October 19, 2025 Xiangyang, Hubei Finish: 3:57 Original post

XIANGYANG, CHINA

On October 19, 2025, Xiangyang turned a marathon into a city report told at running pace. The course moved from a red start area to river bridges, old walls, lantern-lit streets, Tang-style palaces and a finish line that arrived faster than I expected.

I did not come in planning to chase a time. I had not trained in any serious way, and the trip itself had a family-and-work backstory. But once the race started, that backstory moved to the background. The city took over: flags on bridges, actors in Three Kingdoms costumes, kids lining the roads, volunteers everywhere and the Han River showing up again and again.

The race took over before the backstory did

I started in Corral B, wearing a black throwaway layer before the gun and a bright yellow Arsenal jersey underneath. The race color was red, and the start area was a sea of red, so the jersey made me look like a spoonful of scrambled eggs dropped into a pot of tomatoes.

We rolled out from Xiangyang Square and headed south on Changhong Road. Before five kilometers, the course was already on Changhong Bridge. National flags lined both sides, the Han River sat below, and in the distance there were soft mountains. A few performers in Three Kingdoms costumes waved fans and made heart signs at runners while drones moved above the road.

By around 12K, the race was running alongside the old city wall. Red flags were planted along the top, and performers dressed like swordswomen were giving runners high fives below. A little later, the course moved through old streets with stone roads, red lanterns and shops cheering from both sides.

The aid stations were honestly better than many races I have run in the United States. Fruit, snacks, water, enthusiastic volunteers, lion dances, drums - the whole thing had a density of care that made the city feel present at every kilometer.

This was not just a marathon route. It felt like Xiangyang had turned itself into a moving stage.

The course changed scenes like a news reel

  • 0-5KStart from Xiangyang Square, then up to Changhong Bridge with flags, river views and Three Kingdoms performers.
  • 12-15KOld city wall, old streets, red lanterns, kids asking for high fives and spectators close to the road.
  • 20KTangcheng, the palace-style filming location for Legend of the Demon Cat, with drums and ceremonial guards.
  • 26-31KOpen riverside roads and Fengchu Bridge, a blue bridge crossing the Han River with a wide view.
  • FinishA late push, a 3:57 watch time and the strange happiness of realizing the first China race back had gone under four hours.

Somewhere near 30K, sub-4 became real

I had not trained in any serious way for this. I had gained weight during the wedding stretch. The first half of the race had been crowded. So when I quietly checked my watch and realized I had caught the four-hour pacer, I was surprised.

The final ten kilometers were not elegant. I took an energy gel and managed to smear it on myself, turning the yellow Arsenal jersey into a battle-damage edition. But the pace was still there. Near the finish, I realized I could push, followed the crowd in, crossed the mat and saw 3:57 on the watch.

After six years away from racing in China, finishing here felt bigger than the time. It was not just the number on the watch. It was the cultural texture: the old city, the river, the crowd, the local students volunteering, the feeling that a marathon could still make a city open itself to strangers.

The backstory: a wedding, Wuhan and the road to Xiangyang

The reason I was in China at all was personal. Siqi and I were finally doing a wedding ceremony in front of our families. During the pandemic years, they could not come to the United States, so this trip was partly about repairing that missing piece.

After the wedding, Siqi flew back to the U.S. for work. I still had time in China, and I had just joined Midea's U.S. R&D center, so the trip also had a work angle. Then the old habit kicked in: if there is a free weekend, I start looking for a marathon.

Hubei mattered because Wuhan was where running really started for me. I passed through Wuhan first, rode a shared bike around East Lake in the rain and got back onto the Wuhan University campus for a short alumni walk. WHU felt more closed than I remembered, but Luojia Mountain still carried the old training memories.

After the medal

The medal was gold, detailed and proudly local: Xiangyang elements, red patterns, the old city, musical instruments and a green horse in the middle. After the race, I packed up and headed toward the train station for the long route back to Shunde.

On the way, I talked with a Didi driver who had taken her child to watch the race. She said running was a good thing, but kids in China were so tired now that they had less and less time to move. That stayed with me.

I still believe this: whatever policies change, China is never short of people who love sports, and never short of people who want to put on a good race. Xiangyang made that feel true again.
